The lecture had three parts: who does what, how we observe organisms too
small to see, and what mixoplankton imply for marine ecosystems.

## What the microbial loop is

In the classic **grazing chain**, big eats small: diatoms are eaten by
copepods, which are eaten by fish. The **microbial loop** is the other
pathway.[^azam] Phytoplankton leak dissolved organic matter (DOM); more is
released by sloppy feeding and by cells bursting. Heterotrophic bacteria take
up this DOM. Small protists eat the bacteria, and larger zooplankton eat the
protists.

The loop is everything that breaks down and recycles organic matter. It is a
trophic pathway in its own right.

## Who does what

- **Microzooplankton and mixozooplankton** are protists 2–200 µm across. They
  graze on bacteria, small phytoplankton and other microzooplankton.
- **Heterotrophic bacteria and archaea** take up DOM and remineralise it.
- **Viruses** infect cells and burst them (lysis), releasing the cell contents
  back to the dissolved pool.[^wilhelm]

A bacterium has two fates: it is eaten by a protist, or it is lysed by a
virus.

## What the loop does for marine ecosystems

1. **Recovers DOM.** It turns dissolved organic matter, otherwise lost to the
   food web, back into biomass.
2. **Recycles nutrients.** It breaks down particulate organic matter (POM) and
   remineralises it. This releases ammonium (NH₄⁺), which nitrifying microbes
   can turn into nitrate (NO₃⁻). Both fuel primary production.
3. **Bridges trophic levels.** Bacterial biomass reaches larger animals
   through the protists that eat bacteria.

## Observing the unseen

Each new method revealed a larger and stranger microbial world.

**Before the 1980s: plate counts.** Seawater was spread on nutrient-rich agar
and the colonies counted. This gave 10–1000 cells per millilitre. The method is
biased: rich media select the few microbes that grow fast on plenty of food. It
missed more than 99% of marine microbes, which are adapted to low nutrients.
Direct counts under the microscope give about a million cells per millilitre.

**Flow cytometry.** Cells stream single-file past a laser, which records their
number, size and pigment fluorescence. This is how *Prochlorococcus*, the
smallest and most abundant photosynthetic cell in the ocean, was found in
1988.[^chisholm]

**1990s: 16S sequencing.** The gene for 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA)
gives each microbial lineage a unique taxonomic fingerprint, read directly from
seawater without culturing. In 1990, this revealed **SAR11** (named after
Sargasso Sea clone 11).[^giovannoni] SAR11 is the most abundant bacterium in
the ocean, yet it was unknown until then. It is ultra-small, slow-growing and
non-photosynthetic, an oligotroph built for low nutrients. Fluorescence-based
sorting missed it because it has no pigment; culturing missed it because it
does not grow on rich media. It was first grown in the lab in 2002.[^rappe]

**2010s onward: metagenomics.** 16S sequencing needs prior knowledge of what
you are looking for. Metagenomics sequences all fragments of environmental
deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) in a sample, then reconstructs the genes and
genomes present. Mapping these against reference libraries reveals the
**metabolic potential** of known and unknown microbes: nutrient transport,
carbon fixation, energy pathways.

## Small-scale processes, global impacts

Most particle degradation and nutrient regeneration by the microbial loop
happens near the surface. Bacteria, archaea and microzooplankton use DOM and
regenerate nutrients there. This matters globally, for three things:

- **Carbon storage:** what the loop remineralises near the surface is not
  exported to depth.
- **Primary productivity:** regenerated nutrients stay in the sunlit layer.
- **Ecosystems:** the loop sets how much energy reaches higher trophic levels.

The loop also runs through the **phycosphere**, the boundary layer around each
phytoplankton cell. Interactions there are parasitic, competitive or
synergistic. Many phytoplankton are **auxotrophs** for vitamins B12
(cobalamin), B7 (biotin) and B1 (thiamine): they cannot make these coenzymes
and get them from bacteria.[^croft]

## A revised paradigm: mixoplankton

The old split was "plants" (phytoplankton) and "animals" (zooplankton). The
revised paradigm classifies plankton by how they get energy and nutrients:[^flynn]

| Group | Phototrophy | Osmotrophy | Phagotrophy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phytoplankton | yes | yes | no |
| Mixoplankton (protists) | yes | yes | yes |
| Zooplankton | no | yes | yes |

**Phototrophy** is energy from light. **Osmotrophy** is uptake of dissolved
organic compounds across the cell membrane. **Phagotrophy** is engulfing
particles or prey.

Recap from lecture 4: cyanobacteria evolved oxygenic
photosynthesis roughly 3 billion years ago, and chloroplasts descend from a
cyanobacterium taken up by a eukaryotic cell.

### Pathways to mixoplankton diversity

Mixoplankton differ in whether their phototrophy is their own.[^mitra]

- **Constitutive mixoplankton** have permanent chloroplasts and also eat.
- **Non-constitutive mixoplankton** must acquire phototrophy from their prey.
  They are "thieves".
  - **Generalists** steal chloroplasts from many kinds of prey, for example
    green algae (Chlorophyta), cryptophytes, haptophytes and stramenopiles.
    The stolen chloroplasts last only a short time and are renewed by feeding
    every day.

## Summary

The land-based view of food webs is a grazing chain. The ocean is different.
The microbial loop, run by viruses, heterotrophic bacteria and archaea, and
protists, recovers DOM, turns it into biomass, and bypasses the grazing chain.
It is so prevalent because the ocean is so poor in nutrients: recycling is what
keeps nutrients in the upper water column. Its effects reach global ecosystems
and the cycles of carbon and nutrients.
